# No Place: The Officials Who Spoke, and the Ones Who Didn't

*Four cities. Three weeks. $46 million. One question nobody with real power has answered.*

Three weeks. Four cities.

Calgary. A Gurdwara told heavy equipment was coming to demolish it. Edmonton. Two worshippers stabbed during morning prayers. Toronto. A man with a knife targeted Sikhs at two locations in one night. Winnipeg. Swastikas on the parade route.

Every time, an official said the same thing. Every time, it was a different official saying it.

## What happened

On August 15, a caller told staff at Calgary's Dashmesh Culture Centre that heavy equipment was on its way to demolish the building. People were inside for services. A second Gurdwara received the same call.

Six days later, in Edmonton, a man walked into Nanaksar Gurdwara shortly after 5 a.m. and stabbed two worshippers during morning prayers, ages 51 and 75. He had been released from federal prison the day before.

On August 29, a man approached a Sikh man at Toronto's Union Station, made racial slurs, and pulled a knife. An hour later, at Queens Quay, the same man, the same slurs, stabbed someone in the leg. He was arrested September 1 and charged with six hate-motivated offences. Police believe there may be more victims.

On September 5, swastikas and threats were spray-painted on transit signs along Winnipeg's Nagar Kirtan parade route. Hate crimes in the city had more than doubled the year before, from 44 to 112. The community paraded anyway. Twenty thousand people came.

## What they said

The Mayor of Calgary: "There is no place in our city for this hatred or intimidation."

The Mayor of Edmonton: "Hate and prejudice and racism have no place in our city."

The Premier of Manitoba: "Hate and threats have no place in Manitoba."

Three leaders. Three weeks. The same sentence, almost word for word. Each was fast. Each was clear. Each said the right thing within hours of the incident in their own jurisdiction.

Here is the calibrated question, not an accusation, a question: what changed after they said it?

"No place" is not a policy. It's a press release. A man released from federal prison one day walked into a Gurdwara the next morning. What system failed between those two doors? Hate crimes doubled in one city in one year. What happened after the statement, besides the statement?

That's who spoke. Now here's what none of them were asked about.

## The money

Just Peace Advocates filed a complaint with the Canada Revenue Agency on August 31, asking it to examine the charitable status of 21 registered Canadian charities. The complaint, built on data from Sciences Po's Centre de Recherches Internationales, identifies 11 affiliated organizations behind those 21 charities. Four sit at its core: Hindu Swayamsevak Sangh Canada, Ekal Vidyalaya Foundation of Canada, Vishwa Hindu Parishad of Ontario, and Sewa Canada International Aid Inc.

The number at the centre of it: roughly $46 million moving between RSS-affiliated organizations inside Canada, and a further $21.5 million sent from those organizations to RSS-linked groups abroad.

Say this plainly, because it matters: a complaint is not a finding. The CRA has not ruled. Every organization named is entitled to due process, and nothing here proves wrongdoing by any of them. But a charitable tax receipt is a statement by the Canadian government that says this money serves the public. Twenty-one organizations hold that status. On August 31, someone finally asked whether they'd earned it.

## Who they're accused of funding

The RSS, the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh, was founded in 1925. India has banned it five times: 1930, 1947, 1948, 1975, 1992.

Its second chief, M.S. Golwalkar, wrote that non-Hindu minorities in India should live "wholly subordinated to the Hindu nation, claiming nothing, deserving no privileges, far less any preferential treatment, not even citizen's rights." Golwalkar held up Nazi Germany's treatment of the Jewish people as a model for how Hindus should think about India's minorities. That is in his own writing, not our characterization of it.

In March 2026, the U.S. Commission on International Religious Freedom recommended targeted sanctions against the RSS, citing its direct responsibility for and tolerance of severe violations of religious freedom. USCIRF is an independent advisory body. Its recommendations aren't binding U.S. policy. It made them anyway, after years of documenting the same pattern.

## The visit

More than 40 Canadian civil society, human rights, labour, and faith organizations wrote to Minister of Public Safety Gary Anandasangaree. Thousands of individual letters followed. All of them asked Canada to deny entry to Mohan Bhagwat, the RSS chief, ahead of his visit.

More than 100 other Canadian organizations wrote the opposite letter, asking the Carney government to let the visit go ahead and to reject what they called political pressure, arguing any decision should rest on evidence and due process, not advocacy from either side. Two members of Parliament separately called for Bhagwat to be barred.

On August 31, Mohan Bhagwat addressed a gathering in Toronto. He came anyway.

Both camps made their case directly to the people with the authority to decide. Neither camp got a public answer explaining the decision either way.

## Who didn't comment

This is the part of the story that doesn't fit on a slide, so it belongs here.

Three local leaders spoke within hours of a hate crime in their own backyard. That took no political risk. Everyone agrees hate has no place anywhere. Nobody loses votes condemning a swastika.

The two officials who actually held the levers in this story, the Minister of Public Safety who received the letters from both sides, and the Prime Minister whose government those letters were addressed to, are not on the record explaining the decision itself. Not the decision to let Bhagwat in. Not the specific concerns the letters raised. Not the $46 million complaint that landed on the same day he arrived.

That's not an accusation. It's a gap, and gaps are worth naming out loud.

What would it cost the Minister of Public Safety to explain, on the record, what evidence was weighed before more than 40 organizations and thousands of individuals were overruled? If the decision really was about evidence and due process, as the counter-letter argued, where is that evidence, on the record, for the public that has to live with it either way? And if 21 Canadian charities have nothing to hide, what's stopping a public accounting of where $46 million actually went?

Mayors can say "no place for hate" the same afternoon it happens. It costs them nothing and changes nothing. The people who could actually answer the harder question, the one about money, entry, and evidence, have had three weeks and have said nothing at all.
